Reading evolutionary history in sequences

The Evolutionary Bioinformatics Group studies how natural selection, gene duplication and lineage history leave measurable traces in genes, proteins and genomes, and builds transparent statistical and computational methods to read them.

Explore our research Join the group

The questions behind our work

  1. Which evolutionary patterns repeat across independent lineages, and which depend on history?

  2. When is a molecular signal of selection statistically identifiable, rather than an artefact of the model or the alignment?

  3. How can sequence-level evidence be connected to protein structure and biological function without overstating the inference?

Research

We study how evolution writes itself into genomes, genes and proteins, and we build the statistical and computational methods needed to read that record reliably. Four connected themes run through the group's work.

Genome and gene-family evolution

Genomes are reshaped by duplication, loss and the gain of new structural features. We ask which of these changes recur predictably across independent lineages and which depend on the particular history of each lineage, from whole-genome duplications to the architecture of individual genes.

Comparative genomics, orthology and gene-family inference, ancestral-state reconstruction

Molecular adaptation and natural selection

Natural selection leaves statistical signatures in coding sequences, but those signatures are easily confounded by alignment error, model misspecification and limited data. We develop and apply tests of adaptive and constrained evolution with explicit attention to when a signal is genuinely identifiable.

Codon models, lineage- and site-specific tests, codon usage, simulation and sensitivity analysis

Protein evolution, structure and function

Sequence change matters through its effect on proteins. We reconstruct ancestral sequences and follow historical substitutions into structure, stability and dynamics, connecting evolutionary inference to molecular mechanism without overstating what the sequence evidence can support.

Ancestral sequence reconstruction, structural bioinformatics, molecular dynamics

Computational methods and reproducible inference

Every evolutionary conclusion rests on a chain of computational choices. We build open methods for sequence alignment, evolutionary analysis and omics interpretation, and design workflows that report how robust each result is to the choices made along the way.

Sequence alignment, machine learning on biological sequences, workflow design, open-source software

Software

Open-source tools developed in the group, released under open licences.

BABAPPAlign

Progressive multiple sequence alignment with a learned residue-level scoring function, fixed protein-language-model embeddings, affine-gap dynamic programming and codon-aware alignment. Described in Bioinformatics (2026). 

$ pip install babappalign

Source on GitHub Read the paper

BABAPPASnake

A reproducible workflow for orthogroup-centred episodic selection analysis: alternative alignment pathways, phylogenetic inference, follow-up tests and robustness summaries, so each inference can be checked against its assumptions.

People

Dr. Krishnendu Sinha

Principal Investigator, Associate Professor of Zoology

Dr. Sinha develops computational methods for sequence analysis and evolutionary inference. Current work examines duplicate-gene retention after whole-genome duplication, context-aware alignment, statistical identifiability of episodic selection, protein evolution and interpretable omics indicators. He received his Ph.D. in Molecular Medicine from Bose Institute, Kolkata, and taught at Jhargram Raj College in the West Bengal Education Service before joining Visva-Bharati in 2026.

Research fellow

Satwato Goswami

M.Sc. Research Fellow

Intron evolution in the tRNA genes of Asgard archaea, the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes. His first-author study built a motif-free census that detects intervening sequences by comparing each tRNA gene with compact relatives, recovering introns that standard annotation misses, then traced where on the species tree they were gained and why they sit at sites that allow splicing. Methods: comparative genomics, genome annotation and ancestral-state reconstruction.

First author: Hidden introns and repeated gains shape the heavily interrupted tRNA genes of Asgard archaea (bioRxiv, 2026)
