Reading evolutionary history in sequences

The Evolutionary Bioinformatics Group studies archaeal biology, the origin of eukaryotes and whole-genome duplication across eukaryotes. We use comparative genomics and phylogenetics to trace how complex cells and their genomes took shape, and careful computational methods to read that deep history reliably.

Explore our research Join the group

The questions behind our work

  1. Which parts of the first eukaryote came from its archaeal host, which from bacterial partners, and which arose afterwards?

  2. When whole genomes duplicate independently in plants, fungi and animals, do the same genes survive in duplicate, and why?

  3. When can a signal this deep in time be recovered reliably from sequence data, rather than created by annotation, alignment or model choice?

Research

We study the biology of archaea, the origin of eukaryotes and how whole-genome duplication has reshaped eukaryotic genomes, and we build the computational approaches needed to read that record reliably. Four connected themes run through the group's work.

Archaeal genome biology

Archaea are a domain of life in their own right, and the Asgard lineages are the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes. We study archaeal genomes, their gene content and gene structure, such as the introns that interrupt archaeal tRNA genes, and how these vary across the archaeal tree.

Comparative genomics, genome annotation, ancestral reconstruction of gene structure

Eukaryogenesis

The first eukaryote arose from an Asgard archaeal host and bacterial partners. We ask which of its genes can be traced reliably to each source, and what the last eukaryotic common ancestor already had before modern eukaryotes diversified.

Phylogenomics, topology tests on gene trees, simulation, ancestral-state reconstruction

Whole-genome duplication across eukaryotes

Whole-genome duplication has recurred in plants, fungi and animals. Following ancestral genes through independent duplications, we ask how predictable the retention and loss of duplicates are, and how much depends on gene properties or on lineage history.

Hierarchical orthology, gene-family evolution, comparative genomics across eukaryotes

Reliable inference and tool development

Deep evolutionary signals are faint and easily distorted by the way data are annotated, aligned and modelled. We design approaches that avoid circular reasoning and test how robust each conclusion is, and we develop open-source tools when existing ones fall short.

Phylogenetic inference, sequence alignment, workflow design, open-source software

Software

Open-source tools developed in the group. Building careful computational methods remains central to how we work, and new tools will follow the questions we pursue.

BABAPPAlign

Progressive multiple sequence alignment with a learned residue-level scoring function, fixed protein-language-model embeddings, affine-gap dynamic programming and codon-aware alignment. Described in Bioinformatics (2026). 

$ pip install babappalign

Source on GitHub PyPI Zenodo archive Read the paper

BABAPPASnake

A reproducible workflow for orthogroup-centred episodic selection analysis: alternative alignment pathways, phylogenetic inference, follow-up tests and robustness summaries, so each inference can be checked against its assumptions.

Source on GitHub

People

Dr. Krishnendu Sinha

Principal Investigator, Associate Professor of Zoology

Dr. Sinha leads the Evolutionary Bioinformatics Group in the Department of Zoology at Visva-Bharati. His research focuses on archaeal biology, the origin of eukaryotes and whole-genome duplication across eukaryotes, combining comparative genomics, phylogenetics and the development of computational methods, with a particular interest in making evidence for deep evolutionary events robust and interpretable. He received his Ph.D. in Molecular Medicine from Bose Institute, Kolkata, his M.Sc. from the University of Calcutta and his B.Sc. from Presidency College, Kolkata. Before joining Visva-Bharati in 2026, he taught at Jhargram Raj College in the West Bengal Education Service.

Satwato Goswami

M.Sc. Research Scholar

Intron evolution in the tRNA genes of Asgard archaea, the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes. His first-author study built a motif-free census that detects intervening sequences by comparing each tRNA gene with compact relatives, recovering introns that standard annotation misses, then traced where on the species tree they were gained and why they sit at sites that allow splicing. Methods: comparative genomics, genome annotation and ancestral-state reconstruction.

First author: Hidden introns and repeated gains shape the heavily interrupted tRNA genes of Asgard archaea (bioRxiv, 2026)
